Final Project

Each student writes a reproducible statistical analysis of a public health dataset: a Quarto report in a GitHub repository, a 5-minute video walkthrough, and an AI-use appendix. The project is worth 30% of your course grade and is scored out of 100 points.

ImportantDue 11:59 PM ET, Wednesday December 16, 2026

Your last push to your project repository before the deadline is graded. No late submissions are accepted. If you have an approved accommodation, contact the instructor well before the deadline.

Getting started

  1. Accept the assignment: Final Project on Classroom 50. This creates your private repository from the starter template.

  2. Open it in RStudio. In the Console:

    source("https://gph-2182.github.io/gph2182.R")
gph_project()

  3. Pick your dataset from the menu below and write it in your README.md.

  4. Write report.qmd, render it to report.html, and push often with gph_submit() (or commit and push from the Git tab). Every push is a backup.

Choose a dataset

Pick one primary dataset. You may bring in additional public data to enrich it (for example, state populations to turn case counts into rates); cite every source you use.

  • A. US COVID-19 (NYT)
  • B. NHANES
  • C. Gapminder (gm)
  • D. Your own dataset

Daily cumulative COVID-19 cases and deaths for the United States and each state, January 2020 to March 23, 2023, from The New York Times, free for non-commercial use with credit to The New York Times.

  • Files (already in your data/ folder): us.csv (one row per day for the whole country) and us-states.csv (one row per state or territory per day).
  • Columns: date, state, fips, cases, deaths.
  • Watch out: the counts are cumulative, so daily new cases are differences between consecutive days; reporting backlogs make some differences negative.
  • Use the copies in data/, not the live GitHub files, so everyone works from the same snapshot.

A survey-based sample of 10,000 US residents from the 2009 to 2012 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, with about 75 variables on demographics, body measurements, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, sleep, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol.

  • Load it with library(NHANES) and the data frame NHANES (install the package once with install.packages("NHANES") in the Console, never inside your report).
  • Documentation: ?NHANES in R.
  • Watch out: many variables apply only to some ages (for example adults 20+), so missing values are common and often meaningful. The package version has been resampled for teaching, so describe results as patterns in this sample, not official US estimates.

The gm data from the course package r02pro: country-year records of life expectancy, child mortality, income, BMI, smoking, cancer incidence, health spending, and more.

  • Load it with library(r02pro); documentation is ?gm.
  • Watch out: units vary by column (check ?gm before converting anything), and coverage differs a lot across variables and years.
  • You have used gm all semester, so the bar for a fresh question is higher: combine variables in a way we did not in class, or join gm with another public dataset.

Any public public-health dataset you can legally share in your repository, for example from NYC Open Data, CDC data, the WHO Global Health Observatory, or Our World in Data.

  • Approval required by Wednesday, November 25: email the instructor a link to the data and a short paragraph with your question and the variables you plan to use.
  • Files must be under 50 MB each so they fit in your repository.
  • No individual-level data that is not already public and de-identified.

What your report must contain

Your report tells a story with the data. Do not just list statistics; build a coherent argument that answers a question you care about, in a way your classmates probably will not.

  1. Introduction. The question, why a public health reader should care, the data and methods you use, and what the reader will learn.
  2. Packages and data preparation. Load every package in one chunk at the top and say what each is for. Cite and link the data source; describe how it was collected and any quirks (missing values, units, cumulative counts). Explain each cleaning step and why you did it. Show the final data compactly (never print hundreds of rows) and summarize the variables you use in a table or a short paragraph with inline R.
  3. Exploratory analysis. Reveal something that is not obvious from the raw data: create new variables, summarize by groups, reshape, or join tables. Include at least three ggplot2 figures, each making one clear point with informative titles, axis labels, and units, and at least one well-formatted table. Explain every figure and table in words, quoting key numbers with inline R.
  4. Going further: functions plus simulation or modeling. Write at least one custom function with input checks that you use more than once, and use either a simulation (for example a bootstrap interval or a permutation test, Week 10) or a regression model with lm()/glm() (Week 11). Interpret the result in plain language and state its limitations.
  5. Summary. The question, how you answered it, the main findings, what they mean for the reader, the limitations, and what you would do next.
  6. AI-use appendix (the last section of the report; see below).

Every number in your text comes from R (inline code), never typed by hand. A rendered report of roughly 2,000 to 3,500 words, not counting code, is typical; clarity matters more than length.

The video walkthrough

A screen recording of at most 5 minutes (Zoom, Loom, NYU Stream, or similar) in which you present your question, your data, two or three key figures, your main finding, and one thing you learned or would do differently. Show your face or not, your choice. Put the link in your README.md and make sure anyone at NYU with the link can watch it.

The AI-use appendix

AI assistants are permitted for this project (see the AI policy), and every use must be disclosed. For each substantive use, record:

  • the tool, and what you asked (a summary or the prompt itself);
  • what it produced and which parts you kept, changed, or rejected;
  • how you verified the result (checked it against a known value, read the documentation, compared with a second method, tested edge cases).

Include at least one example where the AI was wrong, incomplete, or needed changes, and what you learned from it. If you did not use AI at all, say so, and instead document how you verified two of your key results. Copying output you cannot explain is not allowed, whether it came from an AI or a person.

Grading rubric (100 points)

Component Criteria Points
Introduction Clear question and motivation; data and approach outlined; what the reader gains. 5
Packages & data preparation Packages loaded upfront and explained; source cited and described; cleaning steps logical and justified; final data shown compactly; variable summary. 15
Exploratory analysis Goes beyond the raw data (new variables, groups, reshaping, joins); at least three purposeful, well-labeled figures and one well-built table; insights explained with numbers from inline R. 20
Functions + simulation or modeling A reusable, checked custom function used more than once; a simulation or regression that fits the question, correctly interpreted, with limitations. 10
Summary Question, approach, findings, implications, limitations, next steps. 5
Reproducibility & code quality Renders from a fresh clone with no errors; relative paths only (no setwd(), no install.packages() in the report); seed set once for any randomness; readable, commented, well-organized code; report.html matches report.qmd. 15
Video walkthrough At most 5 minutes; covers question, data, key figures, main finding, and a reflection; clear and audible; link works. 15
AI-use appendix Every use disclosed with what was asked and kept; concrete verification for each; at least one documented AI error or fix, with reflection (or, if no AI used, two documented verifications). 15
Total 100

The rubric was adapted from the UC Business Analytics R Programming Guide final project rubric.
